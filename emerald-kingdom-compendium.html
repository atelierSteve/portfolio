Welcome to the Emerald Kingdom

“Oz is a land of enchantment, where witches and wizards, good and bad, live and work.”

Oz is a land of impossible colors, strange customs, ancient roads, and dangers that rarely announce themselves plainly. Beyond the Emerald City’s green walls, familiar stories become uncertain. Roads wander through enchanted country. Forests conceal creatures who speak, bargain, threaten, and occasionally help. Villages follow their own traditions, while witches, monarchs, travelers, and ordinary folk compete for influence.

The Emerald Kingdom is not simply a destination. It is a crossroads where courage, cleverness, friendship, and a little luck can change the course of a kingdom. Adventurers may arrive seeking a way home, chasing rumors of treasure, answering a royal summons, or simply following a road that seemed to know where it was going.

This sourcebook presents Oz as a setting for fantasy adventures, drawing inspiration from L. Frank Baum’s original public-domain stories while leaving room for your own table’s interpretations. The Emerald City

The Emerald City is the heart of Oz and the destination of countless journeys. Built around the palace of the ruler of Oz, the city is known for its magnificent architecture, bustling streets, and remarkable inhabitants. Merchants trade rare treasures from distant lands, inventors experiment with curious devices, and adventurers seek knowledge of forgotten places beyond the city's walls.

Visitors entering the city are encouraged to explore beyond its grand avenues. Gardens hide unusual plants, forgotten passages wind beneath ancient buildings. Every house on every street in every neighborhood has its own stories waiting to be discovered.

While the Emerald City represents safety and civilization, it is also a crossroads. Heroes, wanderers, scholars, and troublemakers all pass through its gates. Many adventures begin not in the wilderness, but within the city's crowded streets.

The Land of Oz

Oz is commonly imagined as four great countries surrounding the Emerald City: the Munchkin Country to the east, the Winkie Country to the west, the Quadling Country to the south, and the Gillikin Country to the north. Between them stretches a patchwork of forests, fields, mountains, rivers, roads, villages, and enchanted places that rarely behave according to ordinary geography.

The yellow brick road provides the best-known route through the kingdom, but it is far from the only one. Travelers may follow old paths, riverbanks, woodland trails, or roads known only to local guides. Distances can also become strangely unreliable. A journey that should take days might end before nightfall, while a nearby hill may conceal a road that leads somewhere entirely unexpected.

Use the map as a framework rather than a strict measurement tool. Oz works best when geography supports the story.

The Emerald City

The Emerald City stands near the center of Oz, enclosed by a great wall and approached by roads from every direction. Its green towers, markets, workshops, palaces, and streets make it the kingdom’s busiest crossroads. Visitors bring rumors from distant countries, and problems eventually find their way here.

The city is a useful starting point for campaigns: characters can receive commissions from the palace, investigate unusual visitors, seek supplies, or simply become entangled in the latest scandal. Its green glow can make even an ordinary arrival feel ceremonial.

The Four Countries

The four countries each carry their own character, but the borders between them are less political than storybook logic suggests. A forest can divide two communities more effectively than a wall, while an enchanted road may connect places that seem impossibly distant.

The Munchkin Country is associated with blue clothing and fertile eastern lands. The Winkie Country stretches westward beneath the influence of the Witch of the West, with broad fields and rugged country. The Quadling Country lies to the south, where red is favored and strange magic is common. The Gillikin Country occupies the north, where purple is the traditional color.

These conventions are visual cues, not rules. Change them whenever your version of Oz calls for something stranger.

The Wise Scarecrow

The Scarecrow is clever and practical. Though he worries that his straw-filled head makes him foolish, he solves problems through observation, lateral thinking, conversation.

He is admitting what he does not know and asking questions until pieces fit. The Scarecrow prefers peaceful solutions, but he is not helpless. He improvises with whatever is nearby and turns an enemy’s expectations against them. In a campaign, he works as companion, advisor, or quest-giver.

Make the Familiar Feel Strange

Oz is full of fantasy archetypes wearing unfamiliar clothes. A fighter might be a royal guard, a wizard a village trickster, and a rogue might know every road. Keep your class and abilities, but give them an Ozian explanation. Consider the Cowardly Lion: insecurities motivate their growth.

Reimagining Magic

Magic in Oz does not always look like magic elsewhere. A spell may resemble a theatrical trick, an enchanted object, a peculiar potion, or a rule everyone in the village simply accepts. Describe familiar abilities through the setting rather than changing their mechanics. This lets characters remain mechanically recognizable while making the campaign feel distinct. A fire spell might come from a bottle of impossible flame. Teleportation might involve stepping through a painted doorway. A charm could be the right words spoken precisely. These details are cosmetic, but they can make a character feel as though they belong in Oz. Describe the effect first, then explain it only if necessary.
